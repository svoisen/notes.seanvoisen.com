[[Epistemic actions are taken to think, not to progress toward a goal]]. In their paper, Kirsh and Maglio use an extended example of Tetris to show that experts tend to perform more epistemic actions than non-experts. Advanced Tetris players, for instance, will manipulate a piece in space as it falls to see where it should fit, rather than try to mentally envision the various rotations in order to figure out the right location. It is proven to be faster to think via direct manipulation than to think without such manipulation.

This begs the question as to whether it's useful to somehow—via design—encourage novices in a software environment to engage in epistemic actions. One way to do so is to simply lower the anxiety related to "wrong" manipulations by providing obvious safety systems, like simple undo and redo. Another way might be to make such actions easier in general, such as tying common actions that users take epistemically to keyboard shortcuts. ([[Designing for muscle memory means designing for mastery]]).
## References

Kirsh, D. and P. Maglio (1994) *On Distinguishing Epistemic from Pragmatic Action.*
