It's common to assume that action always follows cognition; that is, a person will think about what they should do before they do it (under the assumption they are being deliberate!). But Kirsh and Maglio have argued that sometimes people will [[Epistemic actions are taken to think, not to progress toward a goal|engage in epistemic actions]]—actions they undertake in order get a better sense of the world, but without any pragmatic movement towards a concrete goal—before they think about a problem. Action can thus alter the way cognition occurs (just as much as the inverse) if it unveils information that would previously be hidden or unavailable if the action were never taken. And it turns out that [[Experts manipulate and perform more epistemic actions than non-experts]] in many scenarios (Kirsh and Maglio use the example of Tetris).

This leads to a specific question for design of computational tools: Not just how might a user construct a problem space representation for a given task, but what *actions* could they take to make the task easier? (And thus, could the tool—via actions—more easily change the representation of the problem, or cycle through representations, through a series of actions?) This question might be better answered if we consider [[The fundamental unit of design analysis should be the tool-user system]].
