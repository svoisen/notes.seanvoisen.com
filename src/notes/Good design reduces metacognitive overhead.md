A well-designed interface can reduce cognitive overhead for its users. This seems obvious, but there is some depth to explore here, particularly as it relates to the act of *metacognition*—that is, thinking about thinking.

David Kirsh argues that metacognition is situated in the same way that first-order cognition is situated. This means that a well-designed affordance landscape can help people learn and navigate a new digital space just as a well-designed affordance landscape can help them accomplish the primary tasks in that landscape.

This implies that a design is *good* if it is efficient from a metacognitive standpoint as much as from a standpoint of first-order cognition. Well-designed interfaces help to manage attention and focus users on things that are most relevant or important, leading users through a task (where possible) by removing obstacles that would prevent them from taking a next best step. Examples here include well-designed documents with appropriate headings, or wizards that lead users through the installation of some software.

Kirsh notes that good design which accounts for metacognition can "blur" metacognition into cognition. I believe what he means here is that in a well-designed environment (digital or physical) we aren't really aware of our metacognition—we don't have to stop and monitor what we are doing—so we can better remain in the "flow" of the task at hand.

## References

Kirsh, D. (2005) Metacognition, Distributed Cognition and Visual Design.
