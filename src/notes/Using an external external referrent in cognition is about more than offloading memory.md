It's common for people to create extra representations of problems or other things they need to understand (situations, diagrams, illustrations) even if all the information they need is directly in front of them. Why?

[[Kirsh - 2009 - Interaction, External Representation and Sense Making|Kirsh gives a few reasons for why people create external representations]]. All of these point to a principle of *project then materialize* that seems common in humans. We create a representation, but then modify the world to "materialize or reify" the projection. We use representations not just to reduce our load on our own working memory—or even to simply offload computation—but to understand and accomplish things that would otherwise be difficult or impossible to accomplish without the external referrent. Often the value comes from deeper, broader or shared sense making.

Here are a few examples that Kirsh notes:

- **Having a sharable and identifiable object of thought**: There's value in having something one can point to and share with others to create shared understanding. If all of one's thinking about something exists only in one's head, only to be conveyed through language, then shared understanding in many domains becomes inordinately difficult. Better to have some shared artifact with which to gather around.
- **Rearrangement**: Physical rearrangement of a representation allows us to compare both different statements of a problem and different potential solutions to a problem. It is the reason many people turn to external representations—especially models or simulations—in order to understand, because such representations can be manipulated and rearranged to test different hypotheses in a low stakes or non-destructive way. Additionally, rearrangement often greatly lowers the mental "computational cost" of many explorations by substituting mental work for lower cost physical movement and visual perception.
- **Persistence**: External referrents allow otherwise ephemeral phenomena that need to be examined to be made persistent. E.g. A computational simulation of a short-lived phenomenon like a meteorite impact can allow for pausing time for more detailed analysis. This goes hand-in-hand with rearrangement, because nothing ephemeral can be rearranged in the first place.
