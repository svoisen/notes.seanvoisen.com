Physical environments have an effect—both positive and negative—on human creativity. They also—in accordance with theories of embodied and situated cognition—affect cognition as well.

Tore Kristensen notes that the "scaffolding" for the creative process is the *place* in which creative work occurs, taking into account the space, tools, people and information available. Some environments scaffold thinking and creativity better than others. Environment includes not just the space, but also the activities that take place within it. This is because [[There is a difference between space and place in interaction design]], and as such we must account for the entire context of the environment, including the activities and social context of the activities performed within. Focusing on space alone is generally insufficient.

There is a reason design studios look and function the way they do: the studio space, if designed well, helps facilitate creative output for a team of individuals working physically together. People naturally move around a space, and as they move around a space they may be stimulated by the environment, their imagination primed for new ideas. (Kristensen has an excellent description of how Alvar Aalto's design studio accomplishes this.) Additionally, occupants of studios may bump into coworkers in common areas, conversing, sharing ideas "serendipitously." (Related: [[The opportunity for serendipitous encounters in many digital information spaces is low]].)

When studios have been designed poorly, however, it is often difficult to detect until it is too late—declined creative output and negative emotions are lagging indicators of poor studio design. Oftentimes, imposing too much structure on an environment—extreme symmetry, linear corridors of offices—is a poor design decision. People can't congregate or meet in long corridors; asymmetric spaces may be more challenging to navigate, but also tend to be more stimulating.
